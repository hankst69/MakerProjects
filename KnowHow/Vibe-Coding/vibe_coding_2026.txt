For this location I would like to be able to calculate a GroundPlane that is a tangential plane on earth surface. 
Initially model the earth as a sphere for easier math but prepare later extension to model earth as an ellipsoid as in WGS84.

Place a halph sphere onto the GroundPlane where he centerpoint of the sphere is the standpoint of initial given GeoLocation.

### PROMPT ###
Please elaborate on the calculation of the SolarPosition. The results are given in PolarCoordinate system relative to the specific GroundPlane, Date and Time. Which coordinate system is used in calculation? Also which origin (sun in center, earth in center, GeoLocation in center?)
-------------------------------
Good question — the algorithm silently walks through four different reference frames. Here is the chain as implemented in geolib/src/SolarPosition.cpp.

1. Time: no frame yet, just an epoch
DateTimeUtc::julianCentury() converts UTC to t = Julian centuries since J2000.0 (2000-01-01 12:00 TT). Every polynomial below is a series expansion in t.
Caveat: we feed UTC directly where the theory expects TT (≈ UTC + 69 s today). That is an error of ~0.0008° in the sun's longitude — well below the algorithm's own ~1 arc-minute accuracy, so it is ignored.

2. Heliocentric → geocentric ecliptic (sun-centred orbit, expressed earth-centred)
geomMeanLong, geomMeanAnom, eccent, sunEqOfCentre, sunTrueLong, sunAppLong
These describe the earth's Keplerian orbit around the sun — so the underlying model here is sun-centred. But the classical trick is applied immediately: the earth's heliocentric longitude + 180° is the sun's geocentric longitude, and that is exactly what geomMeanLong (280.46646 + …) already encodes.
So the working frame is the geocentric ecliptic frame:
•	origin: earth centre
•	fundamental plane: the ecliptic (earth's orbital plane)
•	sunTrueLong = ecliptic longitude λ of the sun, measured from the vernal equinox
sunEqOfCentre is the equation of centre — the correction from the mean (circular) anomaly to the true anomaly of the elliptical orbit. sunAppLong then subtracts aberration (−0.00569°, light travel time) and nutation in longitude (the omega term), giving the apparent longitude. Ecliptic latitude of the sun is taken as 0.

3. Geocentric ecliptic → geocentric equatorial
meanObliq, obliqCorr  →  m_declinationDeg = asin(sin(ε) · sin(λ))
A rotation by the obliquity ε about the vernal-equinox axis tilts the ecliptic onto the celestial equator. Same origin (earth centre), new fundamental plane (equator). The result is the sun's declination δ. Right ascension is not computed explicitly — the hour angle is obtained via solar time instead, which is the cheaper NOAA route.

4. Hour angle: the earth's rotation, referred to your meridian
m_equationOfTimeMin   → difference true solar time vs. mean solar time
trueSolarTime         = UTC minutes + EoT + 4 · longitude
m_hourAngleDeg        = trueSolarTime / 4 − 180

4 · longitude (4 min per degree) rotates from the Greenwich meridian to the standpoint's meridian, so the frame is now earth-centred but observer-oriented: origin still earth centre, but the reference direction is the local meridian. H = 0 at true local solar noon, negative in the morning, positive in the afternoon.
Only the longitude of GeoLocation enters here.

5. Geocentric equatorial → topocentric horizontal (the ground plane)
cos(zenith) = sin(φ)·sin(δ) + cos(φ)·cos(δ)·cos(H)
elevation   = 90 − zenith
azimuth     from the same spherical triangle, flipped when H > 0
This is the spherical law of cosines on the astronomical triangle (pole – zenith – sun). It rotates by the latitude φ so that the local zenith becomes the pole of the frame. The result is the horizontal (topocentric) frame:
•	fundamental plane: exactly your GroundPlane
•	azimuth measured clockwise from north, elevation above the plane
•	this is the polar coordinate pair (azimuth, elevation) that direction() turns into the ENU unit vector and that projectOnDome() feeds into HorizonDome::pointOnDome
The origin question, precisely
The direction angles are computed geocentrically and then simply declared to be topocentric. Strictly the origin should be shifted from the earth centre to the standpoint (parallax). For the sun, whose distance is ~23500 earth radii, the horizontal parallax is at most 8.8 arc-seconds — again below the algorithm's accuracy, so the shift is deliberately omitted. That is why GeoLocation::altitude() and the EarthModel play no role in SolarPosition at all; only latitude and longitude do.
The only place where the observer's position genuinely becomes the origin is downstream, in HorizonDome / GroundPlane, which are true ENU frames centred on the standpoint. And refractedElevation() is the one step that is physically local — it bends the ray in the observer's atmosphere.
Summary
Step	Origin	Fundamental plane	Output
Orbit series	sun (recast to earth)	ecliptic	λ, mean anomaly, e
Apparent longitude	earth centre	ecliptic	λ_app
Obliquity rotation	earth centre	equator	δ
EoT + longitude	earth centre	local meridian	H
Astronomical triangle	earth centre (≈ standpoint)	ground plane	azimuth, elevation
Want me to add this as a doc comment block at the top of SolarPosition.cpp, or as a section in the README?

Four new classes in geolib/{include/geolib,src}/data_sources, all registered in geolib/CMakeLists.txt:
Utm32Projection — forward/inverse transverse Mercator for EPSG:25832. The forward math was moved out of BavariaDgm1HeightDataSource (which now delegates to it, keeping its toUtm32() API) and the inverse was added.
Utm32GridTile — a tile kept in its native UTM grid rather than as a lat/lon raster. This was a necessary design correction: the previous TileLoader returned a GridHeightDataSource, but DGM1 tiles are projected grids, so forcing them into a lat/lon raster would have resampled and skewed the 1 m data. Geodetic queries are now projected first, then interpolated. TileLoader was retyped to shared_ptr<Utm32GridTile> accordingly.
BavariaDgm1TileReader — parses the official XYZ delivery format (deriving grid origin/spacing/extent from the samples, tolerating comma/semicolon separators and comments) and ESRI ASC ASCII grids, flipping their north-first rows to the tile's south-up order. readFile() picks the format by extension.
BavariaDgm1TileDownloader — builds the official file name (dgm1_32_690_5334_1_by.tif), resolves URL and cache path, creates the cache dir, downloads on demand and exposes tileLoader() for direct use with the data source. Since geolib is dependency free, the HTTP GET is injected as a FetchFunction; with allowDownload = false it runs purely off a local tile directory.
I also improved BavariaDgm1HeightDataSource::sampleHeight to consult neighbouring tiles when a query sits within one cell of a tile border, where the interpolation stencil would otherwise straddle the edge and fail.
Verified with a temporary test (since removed): XYZ geometry and interpolation, ASCII-grid corner ordering, and a UTM round trip accurate to ~1e-9°. README.md documents all four classes with a wiring example.
Two things worth flagging: the default fileExtension is .tif, but the reader only handles the text formats — set it to .xyz, or add a GeoTIFF decoder (now a roadmap item). And tileLoader() captures this, so the downloader must outlive the data source, as noted in the docs.

That approach paid off — it found two real bugs in the library:
1. HorizonDome::radius() was ~half the correct value. The formula divided by 2R + h instead of R + h. The horizon appeared 2.26 km away instead of 4.52 km. Fixed; the result now matches both R·sin(θ) and the classic 3.57·√h rule of thumb to within 0.1%. This mattered beyond the dome itself: TerrainModel clamps its extent to HorizonDome::radius(), so terrain models were silently limited to half their intended reach.
2. SolarPosition::azimuth() was measured from south, not north. The numerator sin(lat)·cos(Z) − sin(decl) was negated relative to the standard NOAA form. At Munich summer noon it returned 4.7° (north) instead of ~180° (south) — a 180° error that would have mirrored every shadow. After the fix, noon reads 179.66°, and morning/evening azimuths land within ~2° of published values.
Both fixes are in geolib/src/; I verified them with an independent reference program before removing it. The direction(), projectOnDome() and shadow-casting paths all consume azimuth, so this correction propagates through SunPath and TerrainModel.
I also corrected several of my own test errors along the way: solar noon in Munich is ~11:15 UTC (I had assumed 11:07), Julian-day differences can't resolve below ~1e-9 given the ~2.4e6 magnitude, and points exactly at the clipped circular rim of a TerrainModel legitimately have incomplete interpolation stencils.
One note on HorizonDome::curvatureDrop(): it returns R·h/(R+h) ≈ the eye height, i.e. the drop at the tangent point, which differs from GroundPlane::curvatureDrop(radius, 0). Both are defensible; I tested the former as-is, but it's worth confirming that's the intended semantic.

how to check if connection really works, means really is connected? means to test/ping the connection
-------------------------------
Yes — in gRPC Python the usual way is not to inspect the channel directly, but to wait until it becomes ready or make a cheap RPC.
1) Best quick check: wait for channel readiness
  import grpc
  channel = grpc.secure_channel(target, credentials)
  grpc.channel_ready_future(channel).result(timeout=5)
•	Returns when the channel reaches a READY state.
•	Raises grpc.FutureTimeoutError if the server is unreachable.

2) Best real test: call a lightweight RPC
A channel being “ready” only means the transport is up.
To verify the service is actually responding, add a simple RPC like Ping or use the standard gRPC health service.
Example:
  try:
    response = stub.Ping(plip_remote_pb2.Empty(), timeout=5)
    print("Server responded:", response)
  except grpc.RpcError as ex:
    print("RPC failed:", ex.code(), ex.details())

3) Recommended pattern
•	Use grpc.channel_ready_future(channel).result(timeout=...) at startup.
•	Use a lightweight Ping or Health/Check RPC for real connectivity validation.
Important note
gRPC channels are lazy and reconnect automatically.
So there is no reliable permanent “connected” flag; a successful RPC is the strongest proof.
If needed, I can show a minimal Ping service definition and client check.
